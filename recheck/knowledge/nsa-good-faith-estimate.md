# Know your medical bill rights when not using insurance

Who this applies to: people in the United States who don't have health insurance or who choose not to use it.

## The No Surprises Act

The No Surprises Act is a federal law that took effect on January 1, 2022. When you don't use insurance to pay for care, providers generally must give you a good faith estimate of your costs. You receive it when you schedule care at least 3 business days ahead or when you ask for one. You may be able to dispute your bill if it's at least $400 more than the estimate. Emergency care doesn't come with an estimate.

## Get a good faith estimate

- Ask in advance. Tell your provider before care if you're not using insurance.
- Timing: providers and facilities generally must provide an estimate if you schedule care at least 3 business days (Monday through Friday) ahead, or if you request one.
- Dispute threshold: if a provider's bill is at least $400 more than that provider's estimate, you can dispute it.

### What is a good faith estimate?

It's a list of expected charges for health care items or services before you receive them. It isn't a bill. You receive one only if you aren't using insurance for that care.

### What does it include?

- Expected charges for items and services, including facility and hospital fees.
- Estimates cover only one provider or facility. If several are involved, request one from each.
- Providers should include everything they can anticipate, but unplanned care isn't included.

Example: for scheduled surgery, request two estimates, one from the surgeon (covering the procedure) and one from the hospital (covering hospital fees).

### Get it in writing

If you haven't received an estimate, ask for one in writing. You don't need to use the term "good faith." You'll need a written estimate to use the No Surprises Act dispute process. Without one, CMS suggests finding an action plan for your billing situation and submitting a complaint about any violation of the Act.

## Save your estimate and compare it to your bill

1. Save it somewhere safe and photograph it if you have a phone.
2. When the bill arrives, check it against the estimate.

## Dispute a bill

- You can dispute a bill if a provider or facility charged at least $400 more than its estimate.
- An independent third party reviews the bill and determines an appropriate payment.
- Deadline: start the dispute within 120 days (about 4 months) of receiving your initial bill.
